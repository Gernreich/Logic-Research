Modus ponens from P∨Q

Classical modus ponens needs P as its first premise. You can swap in the weaker premise P∨Q and still reach Q. Drawn as sixteen-valued squares, this version gives back exactly Q, with nothing left over and nothing left free.

The result
(P∨Q) ∧ (P→Q) → Q  is a tautology
(P∨Q) ∧ (P→Q) = Q

P∨Q is the weakest first premise that works: every premise that works fits inside it. Classical modus ponens, with P, is one of those premises.

2 · Row by row

Each premise covers the rows the other can't

On the rows where P is true, P→Q reduces to Q. On the rows where P is false, P∨Q reduces to Q. Together they cover all four rows, so Q is pinned everywhere:

Classical modus ponens has only the first half. Its premise P is false on the FT and FF rows, and there it says nothing about Q.

3 · Every premise that works

Eight squares can replace P

Put any square X in (X ∧ (P→Q)) → Q. It is a tautology exactly when X leaves the FF quarter empty, which means X fits inside P∨Q. The eight that work form a cube, with P∨Q at the top. Under each is what X ∧ (P→Q) becomes.

5 · Where it sits

Other names for the same step

Resolution. P→Q is ¬P∨Q, so the argument is “P∨Q and ¬P∨Q, therefore Q”. This is the resolution rule, which cancels P against ¬P.

Proof by cases. Either P or Q holds. If P, then Q by P→Q. If Q, then Q. Both cases end at Q.

Modus ponens as a special case. P fits inside P∨Q, so any argument that starts from P also starts from P∨Q. Starting from P gives the smaller result P∧Q. Starting from P∨Q gives all of Q.
